Stormwater Pond Know-How

Clearing Brush From Retention and Detention Ponds in Charlotte

Why Woody Brush Is a Problem on Stormwater Ponds

Most subdivisions, apartment communities, and commercial centers built in Charlotte over the last several decades rely on a stormwater control measure—either a wet retention pond, a dry detention basin, or an engineered wetland—to hold and release runoff. Those basins are built with compacted earthen embankments, concrete risers, and riprap spillways. When willow, sweetgum, sycamore, and pine saplings establish on the dam embankment, their root systems create channels for water seepage, and when roots decay they leave voids inside the dam. Thick brush also hides sinkholes, animal burrows, and clogged trash racks until an overflow happens.

Working Around Inspection Notices and Maintenance Agreements

Charlotte-Mecklenburg Storm Water Services and surrounding municipal stormwater programs periodically inspect post-construction stormwater facilities, and a common deficiency on an inspection report is woody vegetation growing on the dam embankment, emergency spillway, or inside the basin. If your HOA or commercial property received an inspection letter, share the report with us during the walk-through so we can match our clearing scope to the areas flagged. Because every facility has its own recorded maintenance agreement and planted buffer plan, always confirm specific planting or permitting requirements with the city, county, or your stormwater engineer.

Slopes, Soft Bottoms, and Riprap

Pond clearing isn't flat-lot brush cutting. Basin floors stay soft long after rain, side slopes can be steep, and riprap stone pads at the inlets and outlet will shatter mower blades if hit with a machine. We use low ground-pressure tracked equipment on firm banks and dry embankment slopes, stay off saturated muck that would rut the basin floor, and switch to brush saws and hand cutting over riprap aprons, headwalls, and concrete risers.

Reopening the Maintenance Access Easement

Many HOA ponds sit behind a row of homes with a dedicated fifteen- or twenty-foot access easement leading from the street to the pond gate. When that easement isn't mowed for a few years, saplings and briars close it off so neither a mowing crew nor an inspector can reach the basin. We open the access lane first, clear around the gate, and restore a walkable and mowable path all the way around the top of the bank.

What We Do—and Don't Do—Around Ponds

Our scope is mechanical vegetation clearing: cutting and mulching brush, briars, cattail edges on dry ground, and saplings on embankments, slopes, and access paths. We do not dredge sediment from underwater, regrade or rebuild earthen dams, repair concrete risers or pipes, or apply aquatic or terrestrial chemical treatments. When we clear saplings on an earthen dam, we cut stems flush rather than ripping root balls out of the compacted embankment, which protects the integrity of the dam slope.

Keeping the Pond Mowable Year After Year

Once the woody brush and saplings are gone, your regular landscape contractor can often take over maintaining the banks during their normal mowing visits. For basins that are too steep or damp for weekly lawn crews, a scheduled once- or twice-a-year cutback keeps willow and sweetgum sprouts from turning back into trees.

Questions

Charlotte Retention & Detention Pond Clearing FAQs

We got a stormwater inspection notice. Can you clear what the inspector flagged?

Yes, if the deficiency is overgrown brush, vines, or saplings on the banks, dam embankment, spillway, or access path. Share the inspection report with us during the walk-through so we can build the scope around those items. Always confirm specific compliance requirements and deadlines with the city or county stormwater program.

Do you pull stumps out of the pond dam?

No. Excavating or pulling root balls out of a compacted earthen dam embankment can weaken the dam and create seepage paths. We cut woody stems flush to the ground so the soil structure stays undisturbed. If a large tree is growing on a dam, consult a qualified engineer or the local stormwater agency on how they want the stump handled.

Can you clear brush growing over the rock riprap and concrete outlet?

Yes. We don't drive heavy tracks over riprap or swing a mulching head next to concrete risers and grates. We clear those areas with hand-held brush saws and chainsaws and pull the cut material clear of the flow path.

Do you dredge sediment or repair stormwater pipes?

No. We handle the vegetation clearing—brush, briars, and saplings. If your pond needs sediment dredging, riser repair, or regrading, we can clear the overgrowth first so your grading or civil contractor can access the basin.

What happens to the cut brush around a pond?

On upper banks, access paths, and dry outer slopes, brush is mulched into a ground-covering layer that helps hold the soil. Near inlets, outlets, and emergency spillways where loose material could wash into the riser, we cut and move the woody debris out of the flow zone.

Do you spray cattails, algae, or bank weeds with chemicals?

No. All of our work is mechanical cutting and mulching on the banks, slopes, and dry basin areas. We do not apply any chemical or aquatic treatments.

Can you work with our HOA board and management company?

Yes. We regularly walk properties with board members or community managers, provide a detailed written proposal for board packets, and supply our Certificate of Insurance upon approval.

Can you set up an annual cutback so the pond doesn't get overgrown again?

Yes. Many HOA ponds only need a mechanical cutback once or twice a year to keep willow, sweetgum, and briars from getting ahead of the regular mowing crew.

How much does retention pond clearing cost in Charlotte?

Pricing depends on the perimeter length, bank steepness, how large the saplings have grown, access width, and how much hand work is required around riprap and risers. We provide a firm written price after walking the pond.
